The Spiti Valley Road Trip Guide — Everything You Need to Know Before You Go

Routes, permits, fuel stops, altitude preparation, and the things most travel guides forget to mention about one of the most demanding and rewarding drives in India.

Cover Photo — Spiti Valley
Abhinav Chauhan
November 2024
12 min read
Travel Planning

Spiti Valley — Key Facts

Location: Lahaul and Spiti district, Himachal Pradesh

Average altitude: 3,800 metres (valley floor)

Season: June to October (road access dependent on passes)

Entry from Manali: Via Rohtang Pass and Kunzum Pass

Entry from Shimla: Via Kinnaur and Sutlej Valley (open longer season)

Permits required: Inner Line Permit for foreign nationals. Indian nationals need no permit but must carry ID.

Last fuel station: Kaza (carry extra; stations between are unreliable)

Spiti Valley is the kind of place that gets described in superlatives so often the words have lost their meaning. "Hidden gem." "Last frontier." "One of the most beautiful places on earth." All of these are attempts to convey something that is easier to understand by going than by reading. The valley is high, cold, arid, and vast. The roads are bad. The sky is a shade of blue that lower altitudes do not produce. The monasteries are a thousand years old. And the distances between the things you want to see are significant enough to require proper planning.

This guide covers the practical information for planning a Spiti road trip — the two routes in, the key stops, the logistics of fuel and accommodation, the altitude preparation that most people skip, and the honest assessment of what the trip requires.

The two routes — choose based on your situation

Route 1: Manali to Spiti via Rohtang and Kunzum Pass

The classic approach. Manali to Kaza via the Rohtang Pass (3,978 metres) and Kunzum Pass (4,551 metres) covers approximately 200 kilometres of some of the most dramatic mountain road in India. The road is unpaved for much of its length and requires a capable vehicle — a 4x4 SUV or a well-maintained bike. It is also the more challenging approach: both passes are only open from June to October, the road conditions change frequently, and the altitude gain is rapid enough that acclimatisation becomes a real consideration.

The advantage of this route is the speed and the drama. You can be in Kaza from Manali in a single long day if conditions allow. The landscape transition — from the forested Kullu Valley through the alpine zone to the desert of Spiti — happens fast and the contrast is extraordinary.

Route 2: Shimla to Spiti via Kinnaur

The longer and often overlooked approach. The road from Shimla to Kaza via the Sutlej Valley and Kinnaur covers approximately 430 kilometres and takes two to three days of comfortable driving. The advantage is the season: the Kinnaur route stays open longer than the Manali route because it does not cross high passes. It is often the only road into Spiti in June before Kunzum opens and in October as the passes begin to close.

The Kinnaur road is also remarkable in its own right — the Sutlej gorge between Rampur and Recong Peo is genuinely dramatic, the apple orchards in season are beautiful, and the gradual altitude gain gives your body more time to adjust than the Manali approach allows. If you have the time, this is the better route.

The ideal Spiti trip enters via Manali and exits via Kinnaur — or vice versa. The circuit gives you both approaches and prevents the repetition of the same road twice.

The key stops in Spiti

Kaza — the main town

Kaza is the administrative and commercial centre of Spiti at approximately 3,800 metres. It has the best accommodation options in the valley, reliable mobile connectivity, ATMs (which occasionally work — carry cash), medical facilities, and the fuel station that serves the entire upper valley. Spend at least two nights in Kaza to acclimatise before pushing higher, and use it as the base for day trips to the surrounding villages and monasteries.

Key Monastery

The thousand-year-old Key Gompa sits on a rocky outcrop above the Spiti River, 12 kilometres from Kaza. It is the largest monastery in the valley and home to several hundred monks. The structure is built in layers that accumulate the additions of different centuries, and the interior houses a remarkable collection of thangkas, manuscripts, and butter lamps. Visit early in the morning when the monastery is active with the monks' prayers — the sound carries across the valley in a way that the quieter afternoon visits do not replicate.

Kibber and Chicham

Kibber, at 4,270 metres, is one of the highest motorable villages in the world — though this claim is contested by several other villages in the same valley. The drive up from Kaza takes about an hour on a road that is testing in a small car. Chicham, across the gorge, is connected to Kibber by a cable-stayed bridge that replaced a much more terrifying rope crossing in 2017. The view from the bridge into the gorge below is not for those who are uncomfortable with heights.

Langza, Hikkim, and Komic

Three villages clustered at altitudes above 4,500 metres to the north of Kaza. Langza is known for its enormous Buddha statue looking toward the high peaks and for the marine fossils — ammonites — found in the hillsides around it. Hikkim has the world's highest post office at 4,400 metres, which is a gimmick but a charming one. Komic claims to be the highest motorable village in Asia. All three can be combined in a single long day trip from Kaza.

Dhankar

The old capital of Spiti, Dhankar Gompa clings to a cliff face above the confluence of the Spiti and Pin rivers at 3,890 metres. The monastery is old enough that parts of it are actively crumbling — it appears regularly on lists of endangered heritage sites. The new monastery below the old one is more stable. The view from the old gompa over the river confluence and across the valley is one of the finest in Spiti.

Pin Valley

A side valley branching off from Spiti between Dhankar and Kaza, Pin Valley is less visited than the main valley and correspondingly more peaceful. The Pin Valley National Park is home to snow leopards — though you are unlikely to see one — and the villages of Gulling and Mud at the valley's end are genuinely remote. Allow a full day for the round trip from Kaza.

Altitude — take this seriously

The most common mistake on a Spiti road trip is not taking altitude seriously enough. Kaza sits at 3,800 metres. Kibber and the higher villages are above 4,200. The Kunzum Pass, if you come from Manali, tops out at 4,551. At these elevations, altitude sickness is a genuine risk for anyone who has not spent time at height before.

The symptoms — headache, nausea, fatigue, disturbed sleep, loss of appetite — are often attributed to tiredness from the road. This is a mistake. If you have a headache above 3,500 metres, it is altitude. The treatment is rest, hydration, and if symptoms worsen, descent. Do not push higher if you are symptomatic. Kaza has a small hospital but the resources are limited and the nearest serious medical facility is many hours away.

The practical advice: spend a night in Manali (2,050 metres) before entering Spiti from the north. Spend two nights in Kaza before going higher. Drink three litres of water per day. Avoid alcohol for the first few days. Move slowly.

Fuel, connectivity, and cash

There is one reliable petrol pump in Kaza. Between Manali and Kaza on the northern route there are limited fuel options — carry extra in a jerry can if your vehicle has the range question. Between Shimla and Kaza on the Kinnaur route, fuel is available in Recong Peo and Nako.

Mobile connectivity in Spiti is limited to BSNL and Jio in the main towns. Airtel and Vi do not work reliably. Download offline maps before you arrive — Google Maps works without connectivity once downloaded, and maps.me is a reliable alternative. Do not rely on navigation requiring a live signal.

ATMs in Kaza exist but frequently run out of cash or go offline. Carry sufficient cash from Shimla or Manali for your entire stay. Card payment is not widely available.

What the trip requires — the honest summary

A Spiti road trip requires a capable vehicle, proper altitude preparation, sufficient cash, a flexible itinerary that accounts for road closures and weather, and a willingness to accept that some things will not go according to plan. Roads close. Fuel stations run out. Accommodation in smaller villages is basic. The medical infrastructure is minimal.

None of this is a reason not to go. It is a reason to go prepared. The valley rewards preparation with some of the most extraordinary landscape and most genuine hospitality that India offers. The villages, the monasteries, the sky at altitude, the silence of a cold morning above 4,000 metres — these are experiences that stay with people long after the difficult road conditions have been forgotten.

Plan carefully. Go slowly. Carry more cash than you think you need. And allow yourself more time than your initial itinerary suggests. Spiti does not respond well to being rushed.
