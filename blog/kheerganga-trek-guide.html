The Kheerganga Trek Guide — Hot Springs, Forest Trails, and Why Parvati Valley Keeps People Longer Than They Plan

The complete route from Barshaini, what to expect on the trail, and an honest account of the hot spring that makes the climb worth every step.

Cover Photo — Kheerganga
Abhinav Chauhan
October 2024
7 min read
Trek Guide

Quick Facts — Kheerganga Trek

Distance: 12 km one way from Barshaini (24 km return)

Altitude: 2,960 metres at Kheerganga

Difficulty: Easy to moderate

Time: 5 to 6 hours up, 4 hours down

Best season: May to June, September to November

Base town: Kasol or Barshaini

Hot spring: Yes — natural hot spring pool at the top, entry free

Parvati Valley has a reputation. People arrive for a weekend and stay for a month. They miss buses, rebook trains, and invent reasons to delay departure. The valley does something to people — the river, the pace, the particular quality of the air — and Kheerganga is where that effect is most concentrated. The trek ends in a natural hot spring on a mountain meadow at nearly 3,000 metres. There is nowhere better to understand why this valley holds people the way it does.

This guide covers the full route from Barshaini, the two trail options, what the hot spring is actually like, where to stay at the top, and the practical details that most guides leave out.

Getting to the starting point

Most trekkers base themselves in Kasol, the main traveller hub in the lower Parvati Valley, and take a local bus or shared taxi to Barshaini — the road head village where the trek begins. The journey from Kasol to Barshaini takes about 45 minutes and the road follows the Parvati River through increasingly dramatic gorge scenery.

Alternatively, some people start from Tosh or Pulga, two villages accessible from Barshaini that add extra distance and altitude gain to the trek. These routes are less travelled and more beautiful — worth considering if you have an extra day and want to avoid the main trail crowds.

Two trails — which one to take

From Barshaini there are two routes to Kheerganga: the left bank trail through Nakthan village and the right bank trail via Rudra Nag waterfall. Both arrive at the same destination. They are different walks.

Left bank via Nakthan

This is the more popular route and the one most guides describe. It passes through the village of Nakthan and follows the river closely for much of the lower section before climbing through forest. It is well-maintained and well-signed. Good for first-timers and in wet conditions when the right bank trail can be slippery.

Right bank via Rudra Nag

The right bank trail is quieter, more dramatic, and passes Rudra Nag — a waterfall tumbling out of the cliff face that is genuinely impressive in the post-monsoon months when the flow is high. The trail is narrower and less maintained in places but the scenery justifies the extra attention required underfoot. This is my preferred route up.

The most logical approach is to go up one trail and come down the other, which gives you the full picture of this section of the valley.

The trail — what you actually encounter

The lower section from Barshaini passes through mixed forest — pine, oak, and walnut — with the river audible below. The path is clear and the gradient manageable. Small tea stalls appear at regular intervals selling chai, Maggi, and the kind of conversation that happens naturally when strangers share a narrow trail.

The middle section enters denser forest and the trail steepens. This is where most people who struggle will struggle — the combination of altitude and sustained ascent is tiring if you push too hard. The remedy is simple: slow down. The forest is worth paying attention to.

The upper section breaks out of the treeline into a broad alpine meadow. Kheerganga itself is not a single point but a wide grassy basin with a stream running through it, several camping areas, a cluster of permanent teahouse structures, and the hot spring at the far end of the meadow near a small Shiva temple.

The upper section breaks out of the treeline into a broad alpine meadow — and suddenly the valley is enormous around you, and the peaks that were hidden all the way up are visible all at once.

The hot spring — the honest version

The hot spring at Kheerganga is the reason most people do this trek, so it deserves an honest description rather than the breathless enthusiasm most travel writing provides.

There are two pools — a large communal one and a smaller one separated for women. The water is genuinely hot, naturally sulphurous, and arrives from underground via a pipe that feeds continuously. The setting is extraordinary: you are sitting in warm water at 2,960 metres with the Parvati Valley dropping away below you and snow peaks rising above.

The pools are cleaned regularly by the local management committee but they do fill up in peak season, particularly on weekends. The best time to use them is early morning — before the day-trippers arrive — or in the evening after most of the crowds have settled into their camps. A soak after a day on the trail, with the stars coming out above and the temperature dropping fast around you, is one of the finer experiences Himachal Pradesh offers.

Staying overnight at Kheerganga

Camping at Kheerganga is straightforward. Several local operators run tent camps on the meadow — you pay for the tent, bedding, and meals as a package. The food is basic mountain fare: rice, dal, chapati, and tea. It is hot and filling and arrives without ceremony.

The permanent structures at Kheerganga — there are several tea houses and a few more solid accommodation options — vary in quality. Ask to see the room before committing. In peak season, particularly May and October, book ahead if you can.

Nights at Kheerganga in clear weather are cold and extraordinary. The meadow sits in a natural bowl that catches the wind, so bring a proper sleeping bag even in summer. Wake up before the sun hits the peaks — the morning light on the snowfields above is worth the cold.

What to carry and what not to

Kheerganga is not a technical trek but it is long enough that preparation matters. The most common mistake is treating it like a short day walk and arriving underprepared at the top as the temperature drops.

Carry two to three litres of water from Barshaini — the stream water on the trail is not reliably safe without treatment. Wear trekking shoes rather than trainers; the trail is rocky in places and the descent in particular benefits from proper grip. Pack a warm layer for the evening regardless of how hot the day feels, and carry a headlamp if there is any chance you will still be moving after dark.

Leave behind everything you do not need. The weight of an overpacked bag over twelve kilometres of climbing is cumulative and miserable. One change of clothes, a warm layer, your sleep gear if camping, and the basics is enough.

The descent and the return

The descent takes about four hours at a comfortable pace. Your knees will feel it — trekking poles are genuinely useful on the rocky sections of the lower trail. From Barshaini, shared taxis and buses run back to Kasol through the afternoon.

If you have time, do not rush back to Kasol immediately. Sit on the bridge at Barshaini for a while, watch the river, and let the legs recover before getting in a vehicle. The Parvati Valley has a way of making hurry feel beside the point. That feeling, if you let it, is the best souvenir you will take home.
