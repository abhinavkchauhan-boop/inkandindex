The Hampta Pass Trek Guide — How to Cross from Green Valleys into Desert Landscape in Two Days

Route, campsites, altitude, and the moment when Kullu's forest gives way to Spiti's bare rock — one of the most dramatic landscape transitions in the Indian Himalayas.

Cover Photo — Hampta Pass
Abhinav Chauhan
September 2024
9 min read
Trek Guide

Quick Facts — Hampta Pass Trek

Distance: 26 km over 4 days (Jobra to Chatru)

Max altitude: 4,270 metres at the pass

Difficulty: Moderate — prior trekking experience recommended

Best season: June to September (pass closes in winter)

Base town: Manali

Guide required: Not mandatory but strongly recommended for first-timers

Permit: Inner Line Permit required for Spiti Valley beyond Chatru

The Hampta Pass trek crosses a geological fault line that also happens to be one of the most dramatic landscape transitions you can walk through in the Indian Himalayas. On one side of the pass: the lush, forested Kullu Valley, green and dense with pine and birch, the Rani Nala river threading through it. On the other side: Spiti. Bare rock, dust, cold wind, and a silence that feels geological. The crossing takes one morning. The contrast never stops being astonishing.

This is a trek that rewards preparation and suffers from underestimation. The maximum altitude — 4,270 metres — is serious. The weather can change without notice. And the descent into Spiti, while not technically difficult, is long and exposed. Done properly, it is one of the finest multi-day treks in Himachal Pradesh.

Getting to the start

The trek begins at Jobra, a small village about 14 kilometres from Manali in the Kullu district. Most trekkers hire a taxi or join an organised group departing from Manali. The drive takes about 45 minutes and the road climbs steadily through pine forest before reaching the trailhead at approximately 3,150 metres.

Spend a day in Manali before the trek starts — at 2,050 metres it is not high enough to fully acclimatise, but the rest day is useful for gear checks, buying any supplies you forgot, and sleeping well before four days on the trail.

Day by day — the route

Day 1: Jobra to Chika (4 km, 3 hours)

The first day is deliberately short. The trail climbs from the Jobra trailhead through birch and pine forest following the Rani Nala stream. Chika campsite sits at 3,350 metres in a wide meadow beside the river. The short distance is intentional — altitude gain on day one should be conservative, and the afternoon is better spent walking around the campsite, eating well, and sleeping early than pushing for extra distance.

Day 2: Chika to Balu Ka Ghera (6 km, 5 hours)

The second day is longer and more demanding. The trail leaves the forest behind and climbs into open alpine terrain through a series of rocky switchbacks. Balu Ka Ghera — which translates roughly as "sandy enclosure" — sits at 3,900 metres in a broad glacial valley with the Hampta Pass visible above. This is the campsite where most trekkers feel the altitude most acutely. Drink water, eat if you can, and sleep as early as possible.

The views from Balu Ka Ghera in clear weather are significant. The Deo Tibba peak — at 6,001 metres — fills the skyline to the north. At sunrise the snow on its upper slopes turns a colour that has no useful name.

Day 3: Balu Ka Ghera over the Pass to Shea Goru (8 km, 7 hours)

The crossing day. Start before six in the morning — the pass is more stable in the early hours, and the snow that covers the approach to the top is firmer before the sun softens it. The climb from camp to the pass takes about three hours of steady uphill work across snowfields and loose rock.

You reach the top of the pass and turn around, and the green valley you have been walking through for three days is simply gone. In front of you is Spiti — brown, vast, and completely silent.

The pass itself is a narrow notch at 4,270 metres with steep drops on both sides. On a clear day you can see across Spiti to the Chandrabhaga range in the far distance. Then you descend.

The Spiti side of the pass is entirely different from the Kullu approach. The trail drops steeply down loose scree and boulder fields into a wide, brown, arid valley. The vegetation is gone. The colour palette reduces to grey, brown, and the vivid blue of the sky at altitude. The campsite at Shea Goru, at 3,800 metres on the floor of the Spiti Valley, feels like a different planet from where you woke up that morning.

Day 4: Shea Goru to Chatru (8 km, 4 hours)

The final day is a long, gradual descent along the Chandra River to Chatru — the road head on the Manali–Leh highway. The walking is easy and the scenery is stark and beautiful in the way that Spiti always is. From Chatru, vehicles run back to Manali (about 3 hours) or onward into Spiti Valley if you have the time and the Inner Line Permit.

Altitude and acclimatisation — take this seriously

Hampta Pass reaches 4,270 metres. At that altitude, altitude sickness is a real possibility for anyone who has not spent time at height before. The most common mistake trekkers make is ignoring early symptoms — headache, loss of appetite, disturbed sleep — and pushing on regardless.

The standard advice applies: ascend slowly, drink three to four litres of water per day, avoid alcohol, and descend immediately if symptoms worsen. Diamox (acetazolamide) helps some people acclimatise more easily — consult a doctor before the trek if you want to use it.

The itinerary above is deliberately paced for safe acclimatisation. Do not compress it to three days by combining Day 1 and Day 2. The short first day is not laziness — it is how you avoid being sick on the most important day of the trek.

What to carry

Hampta Pass requires proper gear. This is a four-day trek at altitude with potential snow on the pass even in summer — it is not a day walk from a hill station.

Footwear: Waterproof trekking boots with ankle support. Non-negotiable. The snow on the pass approach and the scree on the descent demand proper footwear.

Clothing: Moisture-wicking base layers, a mid-weight fleece, a down jacket for evenings and the pass, and a waterproof outer shell. Rain is possible at any time in July and August.

Sleeping bag: Rated to at least minus five degrees Celsius. Camp nights at Balu Ka Ghera can drop well below zero even in August.

Trekking poles: Strongly recommended. The descent on the Spiti side is steep and long — poles save your knees significantly.

Sunscreen and sunglasses: The UV intensity on snowfields at 4,000 metres is extreme. Snow blindness is a real risk without proper eye protection.

Guide and porter — do you need one

The trail is marked but not infallible, particularly in the upper sections where snow can obscure the route. For first-time trekkers at this altitude, a local guide is worth the cost — not just for navigation but for safety decisions at the pass. A good guide will read the weather and the snow conditions and tell you when to wait and when to move.

Porters are available from Manali and will carry your heavy gear for a daily rate. If you are carrying more than ten kilograms, hiring a porter is worth considering — arriving at camp with energy left is better than arriving exhausted every time.

The thing the trek actually teaches you

Most people who do Hampta Pass come for the views. They leave having understood something about landscape that is difficult to articulate. The fact that two completely different worlds — Kullu and Spiti, green and brown, wet and dry, forested and bare — exist on opposite sides of a single ridge, separated by a morning's walking, says something about how varied and unlikely this part of the planet is.

The pass is the point of the trek. But the understanding comes from standing on top of it and turning around slowly, looking at both sides at once, and realising that the difference between those two worlds is simply the direction the moisture travels when the monsoon hits the mountains.
