The Complete Triund Trek Guide — Distance, Difficulty, and What Sunrise Actually Looks Like

Everything you need before you lace up. The route, the campsites, the best season, and what the Dhauladhar range looks like at first light from the ridge.

Cover Photo — Triund Ridge
Abhinav Chauhan
January 2025
8 min read
Trek Guide

Quick Facts — Triund Trek

Distance: 9 km one way from McLeod Ganj (18 km return)

Altitude: 2,875 metres at the top

Difficulty: Easy to moderate — suitable for first-time trekkers

Time: 4 to 5 hours up, 3 hours down

Best season: March to June, September to November

Camping: Yes — basic tents and food available at the top

Permit required: Yes — obtain from the forest office in McLeod Ganj

Triund is the trek that introduces most people to the Himalayas. It is accessible, well-marked, and sits at an altitude that gives you genuine mountain views without requiring technical skill or weeks of preparation. But accessibility does not mean it is not worth taking seriously. Done right — with an overnight stay on the ridge — Triund is one of the best experiences the Kangra Valley has to offer.

I have walked this trail more times than I can count, in every season and in all weather. What follows is everything I know about it, written for someone planning their first time.

Getting to the trailhead

The trek starts from McLeod Ganj, which is itself above Dharamshala in the Kangra district of Himachal Pradesh. Most people reach McLeod Ganj by bus from Delhi (about 12 hours overnight) or by flying to Gaggal Airport near Dharamshala and taking a taxi up.

From McLeod Ganj, the trail begins at Dharamkot, a small village about 2 kilometres above the main market. You can walk to Dharamkot in 30 to 40 minutes or take a shared taxi for a few rupees. The forest office where you obtain your trekking permit is at Dharamkot — do not skip this step. Rangers do check, and the permit money goes toward maintaining the trail.

The route — what to expect section by section

Dharamkot to Galu Devi Temple (2 km)

The first stretch is through oak and rhododendron forest on a well-maintained stone path. It is gentle and shaded, which makes it a good warm-up. The Galu Devi Temple at around 2,200 metres is a natural rest point — there is a small stall here selling tea and snacks, and a clear view back down over the Kangra Valley. This is also where the trail starts to steepen.

Galu Devi to Magic View (3.5 km)

This is the longest and most demanding section. The path climbs steadily through forest before breaking out into more open terrain with scattered boulders. There is a teahouse at a spot locally called Magic View — the name earns itself on a clear day when the full length of the Dhauladhar range comes into sight for the first time. Rest here, drink water, and resist the temptation to rush the final section.

Magic View to Triund Top (3.5 km)

The final section alternates between steep rocky climbs and flatter grassy stretches. The trail is well-marked with cairns. As you approach the ridge the trees thin completely and the sky opens up. The campsite at Triund top sits on a broad grassy meadow at 2,875 metres with the Dhauladhar wall directly in front of you and the Kangra Valley spread out behind.

As you approach the ridge the trees thin completely and the sky opens up — and then the Dhauladhar wall is just there, immediately in front of you, much bigger than you expected.

Camping at Triund — what it is actually like

Camping at Triund is the reason to do this trek. The day-trip version — up and back in one push — is fine, but it misses the point entirely.

There are basic tent rental operations at the top run by local families. For a modest price you get a sleeping bag, a tent, and dinner and breakfast cooked on a gas stove. The food is simple — dal, rice, chapati, tea — and it is exactly what you want after a day on the trail. You can also carry your own tent if you prefer, which gives you more flexibility on where you pitch.

Nights at Triund in clear weather are extraordinary. The Kangra Valley lights up below. The Dhauladhar peaks go dark and then blue-white under the stars. The temperature drops significantly even in summer — carry a proper layer regardless of how warm the day was.

Sunrise from the ridge — what it actually looks like

Wake up before five. This is not optional. The first light hits the snow peaks of the Dhauladhar range — Hanuman Tibba, Indrahar Pass, the unnamed summits that line the ridge — and turns them a colour that photographs cannot quite capture. It is not just orange or pink. There is a quality of light at high altitude in the early morning that is genuinely unlike anything at lower elevations. It lasts about twenty minutes before the sun is fully up and everything normalises.

This is the moment the overnight is for. Everything else about the trek — the views, the camping, the trail itself — is good. The sunrise is the reason.

Best season — and the honest version

March to June is peak season. The rhododendrons are in bloom in March and April, the trails are clear, and the days are long. It gets busy on weekends, particularly in May.

September to November is arguably better. The monsoon has cleared the air, visibility is exceptional, and the crowds have thinned. October is the ideal month — crisp days, cold nights, perfectly clear skies.

December to February is possible but the top can be under snow and the trail becomes treacherous without microspikes or crampons. Do not attempt it in winter without checking conditions first and carrying the right gear.

July and August — the monsoon months. The trail turns slippery, leeches are active in the lower forest sections, and visibility is often poor. Possible, but not recommended for a first visit.

What to carry

Triund does not require specialist gear, but it does require preparation. The most common mistakes are carrying too much, wearing the wrong footwear, and underestimating how cold it gets at night.

Footwear: Proper trekking shoes or trail runners with grip. Not sandals, not sneakers. The rocky sections of the upper trail demand ankle support and a sole that bites.

Layers: A moisture-wicking base layer, a mid layer (fleece or down), and a windproof outer. The temperature difference between the valley and the ridge can be 15 degrees or more.

Water: Carry at least two litres from McLeod Ganj. There are refill points at Galu Devi and Magic View but do not rely on them being open.

Snacks: Nuts, energy bars, fruit. The teahouses along the way sell biscuits and Maggi but carrying your own keeps your energy steady.

Headlamp: Essential if you are camping and want to catch sunrise without stumbling around in the dark.

Sunscreen and sunglasses: The UV intensity at altitude is significantly higher than in the valley. Do not skip this.

A note on the permit and the crowds

Triund has become genuinely popular in recent years, and with that popularity has come some damage — litter, overcrowding on weekends, and pressure on the fragile meadow ecosystem at the top. The forest department has responded with a permit system and a cap on overnight campers. This is sensible. Follow the rules, carry out your waste, and leave the top as you found it.

If you want to avoid the weekend crowds entirely, go on a Tuesday or Wednesday. The trail is a completely different experience mid-week — quieter, calmer, and more honest.

The descent

The way down is the same trail in reverse and takes about three hours at a comfortable pace. Knees are the limiting factor for most people on the descent — trekking poles help significantly if you have them. Take your time on the rocky upper sections and let the forest do the rest.

Back in McLeod Ganj, there is a reliable remedy: a large pot of chai, a window seat, and nothing to do for an hour. After Triund, that is exactly what is needed.
